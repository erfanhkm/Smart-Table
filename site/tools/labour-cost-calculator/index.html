The formula, in plain sight

  • Labour cost % = total labour cost ÷ total sales × 100. Both numbers must cover the same period — a week, a month or a quarter.
  • Labour cost per cover = total labour cost ÷ covers served in that same period. It only appears when you enter covers.
  • Count everyone, the same way, every time: hourly wages, salaries, and the employer payroll costs on top (CPP, EI, benefits). A percentage is only useful if its definition never moves.
  • On benchmarks: industry guides commonly cite a labour cost of roughly 30–35% of sales for full-service restaurants, and lower for quick-service formats. That range is a rough reference point, not a target — your format, your province's wage floor and how much your kitchen makes from scratch all move the right number. Your own trend matters more than any published range.
  • The pre-filled numbers are an illustrative example, not a typical restaurant. Nothing you type is sent anywhere or stored — the arithmetic runs in your browser.

Why the percentage matters more than the dollars

A labour bill of $38,000 means nothing on its own. Against $120,000 of sales it is 31.7 cents of every dollar; against $95,000 it is 40 cents, and the same schedule that felt fine in a busy month quietly eats the quiet one. That is why owners watch the percentage, and why they watch its trend: a percentage that creeps up two points over a quarter is telling you something — sales softening, a schedule written for last season's covers, or paid time spent on work that is not serving guests — long before the bank balance does. Part of that paid time is invisible in the moment: the payment relay at the end of every meal. Where to find your two numbers

Your POS sales report gives you total sales for any period in a few clicks; your payroll provider gives you the matching labour total — gross wages plus salaries plus the employer costs on top. Pick one period length (most owners use a month) and one definition, then never change either. Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate my restaurant's labour cost percentage?

Divide your total labour cost by your total sales for the same period, then multiply by 100. Total labour cost means everyone it takes to run the room and the kitchen: hourly wages, salaries, and the employer payroll costs on top of them. Use the same period and the same definition every time, or the trend will mislead you.

What is a good labour cost percentage for a restaurant?

Industry guides commonly cite roughly 30–35% of sales for full-service restaurants, and lower for quick-service formats. Treat any published range as a rough reference point, not a target: your service format, your province's minimum wage, and how much your kitchen makes from scratch all move the right number. A percentage creeping up against your own history is a more useful warning than any benchmark.

What is labour cost per cover?

Your total labour cost divided by the number of covers — guests — you served in the same period. It turns a percentage into a per-guest dollar figure, which is handy when you are pricing a menu, comparing a busy month with a quiet one, or deciding whether a slow weeknight earns its schedule.

Does this calculator save or send my numbers?

No. The arithmetic runs entirely in your browser — nothing you type is sent anywhere, stored, or shared. Close the tab and the numbers are gone.
