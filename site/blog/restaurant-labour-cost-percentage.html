Restaurant Labour Cost Percentage: How to Calculate It and What Moves It

It is the number every owner quotes and almost every owner calculates differently. Here is the formula, what honestly belongs in it, a worked example with every assumption shown, and the levers that move it — including the paid minutes hiding at the end of every meal.

By the Smart Table team · November 19, 2026 · 8 min read

Ask five restaurant owners for their labour cost percentage and you will get five numbers calculated five different ways: wages only or wages loaded, gross sales or net, tips in or out, the owner's own salary counted or quietly excluded. None of them is lying — they are just measuring different things and comparing the results as if they were the same. This guide fixes the definition first, then shows you how to calculate your restaurant labour cost percentage, what ranges are commonly cited, and which levers actually move it.

The formula — and what honestly counts as labour

The formula itself is one line:

Labour cost percentage = total labour cost ÷ net sales × 100

The disagreements all live inside the two inputs. Total labour cost means the full cost of employing your team, not just the wages on the schedule:

  • Wages and salaries — hourly wages, salaries, and overtime for kitchen, floor, bar and management.
  • Employer payroll costs — in Canada, your CPP and EI contributions and WorkSafeBC premiums on top of every paycheque.
  • Benefits — extended health, dental, and any other employer-paid coverage.

Net sales means what you actually keep: sales after discounts and comps, excluding the GST and PST you collect on the government's behalf. Money you remit was never revenue. One more decision to make once and keep forever: whether the owner's salary is in or out. Either is defensible; switching between them mid-year is not.

A worked example, with every assumption on the table

Here is the calculation run once with round numbers — an illustration, not a benchmark; replace each line with your own month's figures.

  • Net sales for the month: $120,000
  • Wages and salaries: $33,000
  • Employer payroll costs and benefits: $6,600 (labelled assumption: 20% on top of wages — your actual load depends on your team's earnings and coverage)
  • Total labour cost: $33,000 + $6,600 = $39,600
  • Labour cost percentage: $39,600 ÷ $120,000 = 33.0%

Two things worth noticing. First, the wages-only version of this same month is 27.5% — a five-and-a-half-point gap created purely by definition, which is why comparing your number to another restaurant's is meaningless until the definitions match. Second, at $120,000 in sales, each percentage point is worth $1,200 a month — that conversion is what turns an abstract percentage into a number worth managing. What ranges are commonly cited — and why to hold them loosely

Industry writing commonly cites total labour cost at roughly 30–35% of net sales for full-service restaurants and roughly 25–30% for quick-service, with fine dining often running higher and counter-service formats lower. You will also see labour discussed as half of prime cost — labour plus cost of goods sold — which is commonly cited in a combined band around 55–65% of sales.

Hold all of these loosely. The sources behind them use different definitions, different markets and different years; minimum wages, rents and menu prices in Metro Vancouver are not those of the markets most benchmarks are built from; and a percentage that is healthy for a tasting-menu room would sink a burger counter. The ranges are useful for exactly one thing: spotting when your number is far enough from your format's neighbourhood that it deserves a closer look. After that, the only comparison that matters is your restaurant against its own trend.

Why the percentage moves when nothing changed

Labour cost percentage has a denominator, and the denominator moves on its own. A rainy week cuts sales while your minimum staffing — a cook, a server, a dishwasher, a manager — stays exactly where it was, and the percentage jumps without a single scheduling decision being made. The reverse flatters you in December. This is why the number should be read over four-week periods, not single weeks, and why a rising percentage is a question, not an accusation: is it a sales problem wearing a labour costume, a scheduling problem, or a workload problem — paid minutes being consumed by work that does not serve a guest?

The levers that actually move it

  • Schedule to the demand curve, not the clock. Staggered starts matched to your reservation book and your POS sales-by-hour history beat uniform shifts. The cheapest hour of labour is the one never scheduled into a dead room.
  • Cross-train deliberately. A team where the bartender can run food and a server can cover the pass flexes with the night instead of standing idle in sections.
  • Attack prep and setup waste. Batching, station setup checklists and a realistic par system cut paid minutes that never reach a guest.
  • Reduce turnover. Every departure restarts the training hours and the slow shifts of a new hire — retention is a labour-cost lever, not just an HR topic.
  • Remove dead time from service itself. Minutes your team spends on pure process — walking, waiting, re-keying, relaying — are paid minutes producing nothing a guest would pay for. The largest single pocket of it sits at the end of the meal, and it gets its own section next.

The dead minutes hiding in your labour line: payment

Timing a traditional payment — flagging the server, printing and delivering the bill, waiting, fetching the terminal, processing each card, printing receipts — puts roughly four server-minutes of work inside every payment. That figure is a planning assumption, not a measurement of your room; we break down where the four minutes go, and how to measure your own, in how much slow payment costs your restaurant.

Run it against this article's example restaurant, with its assumptions swapped for equally labelled ones: 900 payments a month × 4 server-minutes = 60 paid hours a month spent on the payment relay. At an illustrative blended wage of $19/hour, that is about $1,140 a month — very close to one full percentage point of this restaurant's labour line, spent neither cooking nor serving, but carrying paper and plastic between tables and a printer.

The honest framing matters here, and it is the same one we use everywhere: those hours are capacity, not automatic cash savings. Your team is scheduled either way. Removing the relay gives the minutes back to the floor — to the table that just sat down, the section that needs a second set of hands at 8 p.m. Whether that becomes a lower labour percentage depends on what you do with it: serve more covers with the same team, or schedule the same covers with a leaner one. What it never requires is asking anyone to hurry a guest.

Track the trend, not the week

Calculate the percentage every pay period, plot it over rolling four-week windows, and review it beside sales per labour hour — the two together separate "we scheduled badly" from "the room was empty" from "the work itself is wasting minutes." Pick your definitions once, write them down, and let the trend, not a benchmark blog post, tell you whether the levers are working.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good labour cost percentage for a restaurant?

There is no single right number — it depends on your format, your market and what you count. Commonly cited industry ranges put total labour around 30–35% of net sales for full-service restaurants and around 25–30% for quick-service, but sources differ and healthy restaurants operate outside both. Treat any range as orientation, not a verdict: your own trend over time, in your own market, is the number that matters.

Should I use gross or net sales in the labour cost formula?

Net sales — what you actually keep after discounts and comps, and excluding the GST and PST you collect on the province's behalf. Taxes you remit were never your revenue, and including them flatters the percentage. Pick one definition and use it every period, or your trend line will move for accounting reasons instead of operational ones.

Does labour cost include payroll taxes and benefits?

It should. Wages alone understate what an hour of labour actually costs you. The honest numerator is the total cost of employment: wages and salaries, employer payroll contributions and premiums (in Canada, CPP and EI contributions and WorkSafeBC premiums), and benefits. If you compare your percentage against a published range, check what that range counts — mismatched definitions are the most common reason two restaurants' numbers cannot be compared.

How does pay-at-table affect labour cost percentage?

It does not cut your scheduled hours by itself — be suspicious of any vendor who says otherwise. What it removes is the payment relay: the printing, terminal-fetching and receipt-running minutes inside every payment, roughly four server-minutes each on the planning assumptions in this article. That time returns as capacity for tables that need serving. Whether it becomes a lower percentage depends on whether you redeploy it, schedule differently, or serve more covers with the same team.
